# A Spiral Under — book structure

## Recovered seasonal architecture

The published instruction inside “Autumn Prelude” and the author’s recollection of winter storytelling establish this cycle:

1. **Spring — The Biologist**
2. **Summer — The Silent Cartographer**
3. **Autumn — The Philosopher**
4. **Winter — The Ethnographer**

The book follows the calendar cycle from spring growth to winter storytelling by the fire.

The published sentence “The Autumn Prelude should be read after the Prologue, before the Philosopher chapter” identifies the Philosopher as the autumn figure. The Ethnographer belongs to winter, when myths and folktales are told near the fireplace. That leaves the life-focused Biologist for spring and the travelling Silent Cartographer for summer. The old chapter numbers are treated as remnants of the abandoned contents rather than a seasonal sequence.

## Fifth section

The remaining poems should first be tested against the four seasonal speakers. Pieces that resist all four can form a deliberately separate section rather than being forced into the cycle.

Working name: **Loose Leaves**

1. Breathe in the colours
2. Work slips
3. Silence is the smallest poem

This section holds short Notes, formal experiments, occasional pieces, and works whose voice is the poet directly rather than one of the four metaphorical figures.

## Placement principle

- **Silent Cartographer:** a quiet witness who observes atrocities, records destroyed places and displaced lives, and refuses the convenience of looking away.
- **Philosopher:** memory, identity, choice, consciousness, technology, fear, meaning, and contradiction.
- **Biologist:** the poet’s autobiographical self—personal history, relationships, memory, desire, illness, trauma, family, and ordinary intimacy. Bodies, animals, and natural processes are her vocabulary, not the limit of her subject.
- **Ethnographer:** folklore, ritual, family customs, shared stories, social observation, myth, and cultural memory.
- **Loose Leaves:** formal experiments, fragments, jokes, songs, occasional works, and poems that intentionally cross several voices.
